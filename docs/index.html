The problem

The top of the list never changes.

You open Activity Monitor, sort by CPU, and see Chrome, your editor and WindowServer. exactly what you saw last week when everything was fine. Sorting by size answers a question nobody asked. The useful one is what is different from ten minutes ago.

Chrome is always at 30%

Which makes 30% meaningless. The same number is unremarkable on a browser and alarming on a daemon that sat at 2% all morning.

The culprit is often invisible

The usual APIs refuse processes you don't own, 423 of 600 on the machine this was built on. That hides antivirus, MDM agents and WindowServer.

Routine work looks like a runaway

A Spotlight re-index and a genuine leak are the same row of numbers. One ends by itself in ten minutes. You cannot tell which.

The idea

Every app gets its own normal.

CoreBeat keeps ten minutes of rolling history per app and compares each one against its own median, not against the others. Chrome at 30% is unremarkable if Chrome is always at 30%. Chrome at 30% after ten minutes at 2% is your answer.

A spike has to clear three bars to be worth your attention. It must be above 4% at all, at least double the baseline, and at least five points above it. Doubling from 0.3% to 0.9% is technically a departure and entirely uninteresting.

Steady load is never reported as a change. An app pinned at 40% forever must never appear under what changed. There is a test named after this.

The moment it shows up there, the list becomes the same noise as every other monitor and the app has no reason to exist.

The rows add up to the header

Named apps always fall short of the total: a long tail of ~380 processes, plus work macOS attributes to nothing at all. Measured on a 10-core M4 at 56% load:

Where it wentShare

The long tail7.3%hundreds, each a fraction of a percent
Unattributable7.1%kernel_task, and anything that starts and exits between two readings

So the last row is Everything else, carrying the remainder. The build fails if the column stops reconciling with the header, because the first thing anyone does with a column of percentages is add it up, and coming up fifteen points short discredits the number at the top.

Percentages are shares of the machine

An app saturating 3 cores of 10 reads as 30%, where Activity Monitor says 300%. That puts the per-app numbers and the menu bar on one scale, which is the only reason the column can add up at all.

The menu bar

A shape, not a number.

There is no percentage up there. A number invites you to read it constantly and tells you nothing on its own. 70% is fine during a build and alarming while you're typing into a text field. The shape of the last minute says more: a step up that stays up looks nothing like a spiky sawtooth, and a bare number shows neither.

steady under 50%

Monochrome. It disappears into the menu bar, which is where it should spend most of its life.

elevated 50–80%

Amber. Working, and expected during a build or a render. Worth a glance if the Mac feels slow.

racing over 80%

Red, and only with nothing routine to account for it. This one is worth opening.

Two rules stop it crying wolf

It judges sustained load, not the latest reading. The level comes from the median of the last ~16 seconds. Single 100% samples happen constantly, on every app launch and every page load, and a menu bar that flashes red at each one is one you learn to ignore within a day.

Known, finite work is damped to amber. A Mac pinned by a Time Machine backup is working, not broken. If routine background work owns 40% or more of the load, it never goes red.

It says when nothing is to blame

When the machine is genuinely loaded but no app owns more than a third of it, the headline says spread across many apps rather than "nothing unusual", which is technically true and useless to someone whose Mac is dragging.

And before it has a baseline it says so. "Nothing unusual" is a claim, and it isn't made until it has been earned.

The part that matters

A paused app always comes back.

Pause is the interesting intervention because it's the only one that costs you nothing if you were wrong: a compile finishes, a call ends, you resume, and the app never noticed. It's only safe to reach for because coming back is guaranteed.

A Mac left with a permanently frozen app, by a utility you installed to make things better, is a far worse outcome than the slowdown it was fixing. So four independent mechanisms enforce it:

  1. It lapses by itself after 10 minutes, whatever else happens.
  2. Quitting normally resumes everything it paused.
  3. SIGTERM, SIGINT, SIGHUP, SIGQUIT, meaning logout, shutdown and killall, are caught and resume everything first, from an async-signal-safe handler.
  4. SIGKILL and panics can't be caught, so the paused set is on disk. The next launch resumes whatever an unclean exit left behind.

Mechanisms 3 and 4 are tested by actually killing a child process and checking the victim thawed, not by asserting that a function was called.

It never asks for an admin password

Root-owned processes are measured and named, but not actionable. An app that cannot escalate cannot be tricked into escalating, and that's worth more than the ability to kill mds_stores.

Processes macOS needs, like the Dock, loginwindow, WindowServer, coreaudiod, are visible but untouchable, however much CPU they're using.

Install

One line. Then leave it alone.

The installer builds from source on your machine instead of downloading a binary. That's deliberate: locally built apps are never quarantined, so there's no Gatekeeper warning to click through and no paid developer account propping it up.

Piping a script to bash deserves a read first, here it is.

  1. Requires macOS 13+ and the Xcode Command Line Tools, via xcode-select --install. Nothing else.
  2. No Xcode project, no package manager, no dependencies. Six Swift files, built in Swift 6 language mode with warnings as errors.
  3. It adds itself to Login Items on first launch, because a baseline built from ten minutes of history is no use if you have to remember to start it. macOS owns the off switch, in System Settings → General → Login Items.
  4. Uninstall is Quit from the menu, then rm -rf /Applications/CoreBeat.app. Quitting resumes anything it had paused, and nothing else is written to disk.
$ curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/m-arsalan-khatri/corebeat/main/install.sh | bash

Prefer to pull it yourself?

$ git clone https://github.com/m-arsalan-khatri/corebeat.git && cd corebeat && ./install.sh

Run from a checkout it builds the working copy in front of it. ./test.sh runs what CI runs.

Two things it won't do

Ease Off is a brake, not a dial. macOS offers no way to cap a process at a specific percentage, and renice does essentially nothing on Apple Silicon.

CPU only. No memory, disk, network, GPU or thermal monitoring. Stats already does all of that, is free and open source, and the two sit happily side by side. Scope is the feature.

Stop reading the top of the list.

No preferences window. No Dock icon. No account. One menu that answers one question, and a scope that intends to stay that way.
